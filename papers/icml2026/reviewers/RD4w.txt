Our method requires access to a model's log-probabilities, so we are limited to open-source models with publicly available weights. All experiments were conducted on a single A100 80 GB GPU during the review period.

To address the reviewer's concern we ran additional experiments on Qwen3-32B-FP8 (MATH-500 subset, seed=42):

The 32B model confirms the trend at smaller scales: SAGE outperforms BoN by 3 pts (0.97 vs. 0.94) against a strong external RM (SW-Llama, top-ranked on RewardBench), and from a strong 0.80 baseline — the method does not lose effectiveness as model capability increases.

How are aspects determined? Do different tasks require different aspects? What is the impact when aspects are modified?

The aspects are determined by the user before running the method, by writing a judge prompt that describes what each aspect covers and asks the model to produce a verdict in a specific structured format. The judge prompt together with its aspect definitions acts as a hyperparameter controlling which dimensions of quality the candidate responses are evaluated on.

Per-aspect soft contrastive margin scores are weighted and summed for candidate ranking (§3.2.2). Since each aspect is defined through natural language in the judge prompt, targeting a different quality profile is as simple as editing that prompt — no method changes required. Aspects are scored independently, so adding or redefining one does not disturb the others. The same method can simultaneously optimise for helpfulness and brevity, or helpfulness and a warm tone, just by describing those properties differently in the judge prompt.

Why was GPT-4.1 used for prompt tuning when implementing SPO?

To avoid deviating from the original method, we followed the SPO authors' library (MetaGPT), which recommends GPT-4o for prompt optimisation. The library's templates work well with GPT-4* models but transfer poorly to Qwen. Rather than modifying SPO's setup (which could bias the comparison) we used the most recent compatible GPT model as specified by the original implementation.

Group-wise comparisons claim lacks experimental proof. How should m_min be selected?

Using only the single best and worst candidate (group size 1) is often suboptimal: no scoring signal is perfect, and a genuinely good response can occasionally rank below a weaker one. With groups this small, the textual gradient becomes noisy and convergence suffers.

We recommend 1 < m_min < N/2. In our experiments m_min=3 works well across tasks — on mathematical problems the initial top-3 hit rate is 0.93 (Figure 5b), so a positive group of that size reliably contains high-quality generations.

What are the differences in cost and time between the proposed method and other baselines?

We ran evaluations on MATH-500 subset (A100 80 GB). SAGE outperforms BoN when matched at N=7 and also when BoN is given enough budget to match SAGE's wall-clock time. SAGE with the 1.7B model uses ~5.7× less VRAM than BoN+8B RM. For TPO: ~80% ±5% of SAGE's wall-clock at default settings.
