Summary:
This paper presents SAGE, a test-time alignment method for large language models.

SAGE discards external reward models and lets the base LLM act as both generator and “self-judge”.

It computes continuous preference scores using calibrated contrastive labels, splits good/bad candidate sets to extract a “text gradient”, and uses this text instruction to guide subsequent iterative generation and optimization.

Strengths And Weaknesses:
Strengths

The method does not rely on external reward models, effectively reducing system complexity and avoiding the risk of reward hacking.

Extracting text gradients via grouped comparisons is more stable than traditional “best vs. worst” comparisons, reducing noise and outliers.

Weaknesses

The method heavily depends on the model’s own evaluation and reflection ability. For small-scale models with weak self-evaluation capabilities, the extracted text gradient may be invalid.

Multi-turn iterative generation and self-judgment still incur linear inference computation and time costs, limiting practicality in latency-sensitive real-time interaction scenarios.

We would like to thank the reviewer for their thoughtful feedback and comments. We are glad the reviewer appreciated the stability of group-wise gradient extraction and the simplicity of keeping the system free of external reward models. Below we have answered the questions. We denote the following abbreviations:

FsfairX = sfairXC/FsfairX-LLaMA3-RM-v0.1;
SW-Qwen3 = Skywork/Skywork-Reward-V2-Qwen3-8B;

The method heavily depends on the model's own evaluation and reflection ability. For small-scale models with weak self-evaluation capabilities, the extracted text gradient may be invalid. For small-parameter models with poor self-evaluation capabilities (such as those below 3B), will the "text gradient" generated by SAGE become noise and interfere with the generation quality?

To address this concern we ran additional experiments on MATH-500 subset using Qwen3-1.7B — a model twice smaller than the 3B threshold. We evaluate SAGE against Best-of-N with multiple reward models and observe that SAGE on a 1.7B generator substantially outperforms BoN even when BoN is paired with larger (8B) reward models and a much higher sample count.

We also run an important ablation: replacing the iterative refinement with pure ranking via SAGE's own contrastive margin scores (BoN with the same SAGE judge, no textual gradient applied). This scores 0.78 at N=7, while SAGE with 1 optimization epoch reaches 0.84 at the same N=7. This gap indicates that the textual gradient actively pushes new generations toward improvement beyond what better selection alone could achieve.

Multi-turn iterative generation and self-judgment still incur linear inference computation and time costs, limiting practicality in latency-sensitive real-time interaction scenarios. Please provide performance comparison data between SAGE and traditional Best-of-N sampling under the same end-to-end latency to verify its computational efficiency in practical applications.

To answer this, we ran a series of wall-clock time measurements on MATH-500 subsets using Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-8B. All experiments were conducted using an A100 GPU with 80GB VRAM. We compare SAGE against Best-of-N in two setups: first with a fixed N=7 for BoN (matching the number of candidates per SAGE epoch), and then with N scaled up so that the total wall-clock time for BoN approximately matches that of SAGE.

We observe that SAGE consistently outperforms BoN in both setups. The resource efficiency argument is especially clear for the 1.7B model: SAGE using only 1.7B requires less VRAM yet achieves higher accuracy than BoN paired with an 8B reward model — a ~5.7× VRAM saving (since (1.7 + 8) / 1.7 ≈ 5.7) while still delivering better results.
