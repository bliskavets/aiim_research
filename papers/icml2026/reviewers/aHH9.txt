Summary:
SAGE is a test-time alignment method that enables Large Language Models (LLMs) to refine their own outputs during inference without requiring additional training or external reward models. The system uses the base LLM as a "soft judge" by calculating a calibrated contrastive label margin from log-probabilities to serve as a continuous preference signal. It introduces an iterative algorithm that groups candidates into "good" and "bad" sets to extract a stable textual "gradient" for guiding subsequent generations.

Strengths And Weaknesses:
Strengths and Weaknesses
Strengths
Self-Sufficient Alignment: Moving away from external reward models (RMs) reduces system complexity and avoids the "proxy mismatch" where the RM is misaligned with the generator.
Robust Feedback Heuristics: Using grouped selection (good sets vs. bad sets) rather than extreme-only comparisons makes the textual guidance much more stable and less sensitive to outliers.
Continuous Signal: Converting discrete judge tags into a calibrated log-probability margin provides a fine-grained preference signal that supports better ranking than simple voting.
Weaknesses
Iterative refinement is a token hog. While the authors claim it’s "resource-lean," the sequential nature of generating (N) hypotheses over (T) epochs means high latency that might not fly in real-time applications.
If the base model isn't smart enough to get the math right, can we really trust its internal "confidence" to judge it correctly?. There’s a risk of the judge simply reinforcing the model's own hallucinations.
he "textual gradient" is just a summarization of differences. Its effectiveness depends entirely on the LLM’s ability to reflect, which varies wildly across different model scales and domains.
The gains are most impressive on MATH-500. For more subjective tasks like AlpacaEval, the win rates might be partly driven by the model simply learning to be more "verbose" or "helpful-sounding" rather than actually better.
Soundness: 3: good
Presentation: 3: good
Significance: 2: fair
Originality: 3: good

We would like to thank the reviewer for their thoughtful feedback and comments. We are pleased the reviewer recognised the self-sufficient nature of the design — in particular, how a calibrated log-probability margin sidesteps the proxy mismatch that external reward models typically introduce. Below we have answered the questions. Please don't hesitate to let us know if anything was unclear or if you require additional clarification.

Can you provide a wall-clock breakdown for SAGE vs. Best-of-N? If the improvement takes 3x longer, is it worth it?

We ran wall-clock measurements on MATH-500 (A100 80 GB) for Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-8B across three RMs. SAGE consistently outperforms BoN at fixed N and at time-matched N. For the 1.7B model, SAGE requires ~5.7× less VRAM than BoN+8B RM yet achieves higher accuracy.

If the base model isn't smart enough to get math right, can we really trust its internal confidence to judge correctly?

While our main experiments use 8B models as a practical balance between size and self-assessment quality, we find that even substantially smaller models retain meaningful self-evaluation ability. We ran Qwen3-1.7B on MATH-500, comparing SAGE's contrastive margin scores against three larger (8B) RMs:

Method	Accuracy
Baseline (greedy, t=0.0)	0.56
BoN (FsfairX, N=21)	0.62
BoN (SW-Qwen3, N=21)	0.68
BoN (SW-Llama, N=21)	0.84
BoN (SAGE contrastive scores, N=7)	0.78
Only SW-Llama achieves higher ranking accuracy. Yet SAGE with 2 epochs (0.88) still surpasses that best BoN+RM result (0.86), showing iterative refinement brings gains beyond what any ranking method achieves alone.

The "textual gradient" is just a summarization of differences — effectiveness varies wildly across scales and domains.

We agree reflective quality varies; we don't claim uniformity. It is consistently useful in practice: (1) experiments on Qwen and Llama families show similar relative gains on both architectures; (2) Qwen3-1.7B still yields meaningful and actionable gradients — accuracy improves from 0.56 to 0.88 over 2 epochs, confirming the gradients are not noise.

AlpacaEval wins may be partly driven by verbosity rather than quality.

In Table 1 we report LC-WinRate [1] (62.99 for Qwen, 47.1 for Llama), which explicitly corrects for length bias — confirming wins are not verbosity-driven. Supplementary experiments on MMLU-Pro STEM and IFEval subsets, where length is irrelevant, further support this:

Have you tested this on larger models (e.g., 70B+)?

We ran Qwen3-32B-FP8 on MATH-500 subset (seed=42):

Method	Accuracy
Baseline (greedy, t=0.0)	0.80
BoN (SW-Llama, N=7)	0.94

SAGE outperforms BoN+strong RM by 3 pts (0.97 vs. 0.94) from a strong 0.80 baseline.

Does SAGE ever get stuck in a "self-congratulatory" loop where the gradient reinforces incorrect but consistent reasoning?

We have not observed self-reinforcing loops. The initial RM score dip (Fig. 4b) is due to generator-RM distribution mismatch, which resolves as optimisation progresses. To support this, we tracked both metrics across epochs on a MATH-500 subset with Qwen3-1.7B, using Skywork/Skywork-Reward-V2-Qwen3-8B as the reward model. Since both models come from the Qwen family, the overlap in their training data is presumably large:

Epoch	Mean RM score	Mean SAGE score
Initial	+4.40	+5.91
Epoch 0	+8.36	+10.82
Epoch 1	+9.43	+12.96
Pearson correlation >0.99 — both metrics improve monotonically illustrating that models trained on similar data can evaluate each other's generations more reliably. Using the model's own contrastive scores (as SAGE does) naturally avoids RM distribution mismatch.
